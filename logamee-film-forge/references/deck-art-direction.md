# Deck Art Direction

This is the taste layer for Logamee Film Forge's `deck` mode. It describes how
to make a page feel authored, intelligent, and memorable rather than merely
correct. It is intentionally separate from the visual standard:

- the visual standard protects readability, consistency, and production safety;
- art direction determines what to emphasize, what to omit, and what kind of
  visual character the page should leave behind.

Do not turn these principles into a fixed template. Taste is visible in the
quality of decisions, not in the repetition of one recognizable layout.

## The Position

The deck should feel like a carefully edited visual essay: clear enough to
teach, composed enough to reward looking, and human enough to have a point of
view.

It should not feel like:

- a SaaS dashboard assembled from cards;
- a poster covered with decoration;
- a children's worksheet;
- a collection of unrelated style experiments;
- a transcript placed on top of a background;
- a motion-effects demo searching for a reason to move.

The desired character is **editorial clarity with human warmth**:
disciplined hierarchy, generous space, one memorable gesture, and enough
irregularity to show that a person made a choice.

## 1. Begin With a Point of View

Every page needs a design sentence before it needs a layout:

> “This page should make the viewer feel/understand **X** by seeing **Y**.”

Examples:

- A complex system should feel graspable because its parts visibly gather around
  one governing idea.
- A contrast should feel consequential because the two sides occupy different
  visual weight, not merely different columns.
- A difficult first step should feel approachable because distance is reduced
  to one visible next move.
- A screenshot should feel like evidence because the composition leads the eye
  to the relevant detail rather than presenting a tiny browser window.

If the design sentence could describe any page in the deck, it is too vague.
If it only names the topic, it is not a point of view.

## 2. Compose, Do Not Populate

A page is a composition of forces, not a set of UI components.

- Decide the dominant shape, direction, scale, or contrast before placing
  secondary objects.
- Let related things share space, alignment, or a visible relation instead of
  giving each one its own container.
- Allow one element to carry visual weight while nearby elements support it.
- Treat every added line, badge, border, icon, and caption as a claim on the
  viewer's attention.
- If removing an element makes the page clearer without weakening the idea,
  remove it.

The default question is not “What can we add to fill this area?” It is “What
is the strongest arrangement for this idea?”

## 3. Use One Decisive Gesture

Each page should have one memorable visual move. It may be:

- a large crop that creates scale or tension;
- a single smooth hand-drawn contour that groups the idea;
- a path that visibly carries a concept from one state to another;
- an unexpected alignment or interruption in an otherwise calm grid;
- a change of scale that makes one relationship impossible to miss;
- a transformation in which one form becomes another.

The gesture must clarify or frame the idea. It is not an ornament collection.
Do not combine several signature devices on one page. A strong page often has
one unusual decision surrounded by quiet, ordinary decisions.

Human irregularity is allowed when it looks intentional: a confident curve,
slight offset, or imperfect enclosure. It must remain simple and fluid. Wobbly
loops, tangled connector lines, fake roughness, and random distortion are not
human character; they are noise.

## 4. Build Tension Between Calm and Emphasis

Good educational pages do not shout everywhere. They establish a quiet field
and then make one change matter.

- Keep most of the canvas visually calm.
- Concentrate contrast, saturation, scale, or motion at the current idea.
- Let inactive context remain legible but less demanding.
- Use a pause, gap, or unoccupied region before a meaningful reveal.
- Reserve the strongest treatment for the conclusion or the currently spoken
  object, not for every object at once.

The viewer should feel that the page knows where to look. If everything is
bright, outlined, animated, or equally large, the page has no taste even when
it is technically tidy.

## 5. Make Asymmetry Earn Its Place

Symmetry is useful for stability; asymmetry is useful for attention. Neither is
automatically sophisticated.

Use asymmetry when it creates:

- a clear reading direction;
- a productive imbalance between cause and result;
- room for an image, route, or gesture to breathe;
- a difference in importance or time;
- a deliberate pause on one side of the frame.

Do not scatter elements to imitate editorial design. A composition may be
uneven, but its visual weight, alignment, and destination must still be
understandable.

## 6. Let Typography Carry Voice

Typography is not a label layer added after the drawing. It is one of the
primary visual materials.

- Give the main claim enough scale and authority to lead the page.
- Use short, deliberate wording on the canvas; let narration carry the full
  explanation.
- Let line breaks express meaning, emphasis, or pacing rather than occur only
  because a box ran out of width.
- Keep one typographic voice across the deck and vary hierarchy, not identity.
- Treat English, code, numbers, and Chinese as parts of one composition; do not
  let fallback fonts silently produce a second visual personality.

Avoid decorative typography that competes with the idea: excessive outlines,
random uppercase labels, noisy tracking, novelty fonts, and multiple competing
headline treatments.

## 7. Treat Images as Evidence or Atmosphere, Never Filler

An image must change what the viewer understands or feels.

- Evidence deserves inspection, proximity, and a useful crop.
- Demonstration deserves a readable stage and a clear pointer to the action.
- Identity deserves scale and a strong entrance into the visual world.
- Atmosphere deserves restraint and must not compete with the claim.

Do not place a screenshot inside a generic card merely to make it look
designed. Design the crop, surrounding space, annotation, and relationship to
the spoken sentence. A URL or access instruction should belong to that same
composition; it should not be demoted to a decorative footer strip.

Before sizing a screenshot, decide whether the viewer needs to inspect it or
only recognize it as proof. If it is proof, the claim or next action should
remain the first read; do not let a large screenshot of a website turn the
slide into a browser landing page. If the screenshot itself is the subject,
make that the page's explicit point of view and remove competing focal claims.

## 8. Give the Page a Visual Sentence

The viewer should be able to describe the page as an action or relationship,
not only as a list of nouns:

- “The scattered ideas are being gathered into one path.”
- “The old assumption is losing weight while the new model takes its place.”
- “This image is the evidence, and the mark directs me to the relevant detail.”
- “The three capabilities are different entry points into the same learning
  system.”

If the best paraphrase is “there are three cards with three labels,” the page
has not yet become a visual sentence. Change the geometry before polishing it.

## 9. Vary the Composition, Keep the Voice

The deck should have a recognizable author, not a repeated template.

Keep stable:

- the typographic voice;
- the meaning of colors and emphasis;
- the behavior of line, shape, and texture;
- the attitude toward space;
- the level of visual restraint.

Vary:

- the dominant relation;
- the scale and crop;
- the direction of movement;
- the balance of image, geometry, and text;
- the location of the focal anchor.

Consistency is a shared grammar. Repetition is using the same sentence for
every idea.

## 10. Animate the Change in Understanding

In `deck` mode, animation is the movement of attention and meaning between
states. It should answer:

- What was not understood before?
- What becomes visible, connected, separated, or reweighted now?
- Why does this change happen at this spoken moment?
- What should remain in the viewer's mind when the motion settles?

Prefer one meaningful transformation over a parade of entrances. A page may
start quiet, reveal a relation, shift focus, and settle. It should not make
every object fly, bounce, scale, and glow merely to prove that it is animated.

The best motion often leaves a clearer still frame behind it.

## 11. Taste Tests

Use these as judgment prompts, not as a mechanical score:

1. **Author test:** can the designer explain the main visual decision in one
   sentence?
2. **Subtraction test:** what can disappear without weakening the idea?
3. **First-glance test:** does the eye know where to begin?
4. **Silent-geometry test:** with labels hidden, is the main relation still
   suggested?
5. **Signature test:** is there one memorable move, rather than many tricks?
6. **Voice test:** does the page belong to this deck and not to a random
   template library?
7. **Afterimage test:** after looking away, what single shape, contrast, or
   relationship remains?
8. **Motion test:** does the animation make the idea clearer, or merely make the
   page busier?
9. **Role test:** can a viewer distinguish the claim, evidence, and next action
   at a glance, with no supporting item accidentally taking over?
10. **Neighbor test:** when compared with the previous and next rendered page,
    does this feel like a new composition in the same deck rather than a new
    template?

When a page fails these tests, revise the composition and visual claim before
adding more decoration, animation, or explanatory text.

## Rejection Patterns

Reject a page that relies on:

- centered title + three equal cards as an automatic answer;
- equal visual weight for unequal ideas;
- decorative lines that do not express a relation;
- hand-drawn noise mistaken for warmth;
- screenshots reduced to thumbnails beside a wall of text;
- several accents competing for attention;
- empty space that is only leftover space;
- a new visual style introduced to disguise weak content;
- a supporting screenshot dominating the frame while the page's claim or
  action becomes small or hard to find;
- essential copy floating independently of the image or idea it explains;
- a new page that changes the deck's typographic or spacing system without a
  deliberate, deck-wide reason;
- animation that reveals everything before the narration reaches it.
